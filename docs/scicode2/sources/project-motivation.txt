Motivation
Current coding systems are increasingly capable of implementing prescribed numerical methods and operating existing scientific workflows. These capabilities are useful, but they address only one part of scientific computation.
In research, the computational procedure is often not specified in advance. A scientist may know the system to study, the observable to calculate, and the level of accuracy required without knowing (or without explicitly stating) the full sequence of computational choices needed to obtain a reliable answer. Determining that procedure is itself part of the scientific work.
A researcher may need to decide how to formulate the problem, which physical or biological effects matter, which approximations are justified, what numerical method is appropriate, how to balance accuracy and computational cost, what diagnostics reveal whether an approach is working, and what evidence is sufficient to trust the final result. These decisions are often more scientifically meaningful than writing the code that executes them.
This distinction becomes increasingly important as AI systems become more autonomous. A system that requires a human researcher to specify the physical model, approximation, discretization, solver, convergence criteria, and validation procedure can greatly accelerate execution, but the scientific procedure it follows is still largely human-designed. A more capable system must be able to translate a scientific objective into an appropriate computational procedure, execute it, diagnose its limitations, revise its approach when necessary, and establish that its outputs are trustworthy.
SciCode 2 is intended to evaluate this transition from executing a scientific workflow to constructing a scientifically justified one.
A central target is the current frontier of human-AI collaboration. We are interested in problems that frontier AI systems cannot yet solve reliably on their own, but that a capable scientist can bring to a validated solution in roughly 10-20 hours when working with current frontier tools such as Claude Code or Codex. During that process, the scientist may need to inspect intermediate results, identify invalid assumptions, redirect the computational strategy, introduce new diagnostics, or decide what calculation should be performed next.
This suggests a more useful definition of long-horizon scientific work. Traditionally, task difficulty is often described by how long a scientist would take to complete the work independently. As AI tools become part of scientific practice, a more relevant frontier is how much expert time remains necessary when the scientist has access to the best available AI systems. SciCode 2 therefore measures the scientific judgment that cannot yet be delegated to AI, rather than simply the total amount of implementation, computation, or unaided human work required.
Creating a SciCode 2 Problem
A SciCode 2 task should begin with a scientific objective and practical constraints rather than a prescribed computational recipe. The system should be told what scientific result is needed, but not exactly how to obtain it. It must determine the substantive steps itself: how to formulate the problem computationally, what assumptions and approximations are appropriate, which methods to use, how to diagnose failures, and how to establish that the final result is reliable.
The result should ideally be new and non-searchable. Rather than asking the model to reproduce a published number or a standard textbook calculation, the task should involve a newly constructed, modified, or previously unanalyzed system. This makes it difficult to succeed through retrieval alone and forces the system to carry out the scientific work.
The difficulty should come from scientific judgment, not primarily from generic software engineering. A strong task should contain points where the solver has to make meaningful domain-specific decisions based on intermediate results. An initial approximation may turn out to be inadequate, a numerical method may fail in a particular regime, or an unexpected result may require an additional diagnostic. Different scientifically reasonable approaches may therefore lead to the answer, and we do not want to require a model to reproduce one predetermined reference workflow.
At the same time, every task must be demonstrably solvable. The contributor should produce at least one validated reference solution and provide the information required for another capable scientist to solve the task independently. The computational requirements should also remain practical: substantial computation is acceptable, including modest GPU use, but the benchmark should not depend on access to a large cluster for days or weeks.
Evaluation should reflect how scientists actually establish that a result is correct. Depending on the domain, this might involve numerical targets, conservation laws, physical invariants, limiting cases, convergence behavior, statistical properties, agreement between independent methods, qualitative features of a phase diagram, or other domain-specific checks. Wherever possible, these criteria should be converted into robust automated tests while still allowing multiple valid solution strategies.
The target difficulty is deliberately demanding. We want problems that current frontier systems fail to solve reliably in autonomous runs, but that an expert scientist using systems such as Claude Code or Codex can solve with roughly 10–20 hours of active interaction and scientific guidance. In this sense, the benchmark is intended to measure the gap between what AI can execute independently and what becomes possible when a scientist provides the missing judgment.
