# Lattice Gap Mechanism from Experiment

## Background and motivation

Chang et al. study a bilayer square-lattice fermion model whose strong-coupling ground state is gapped and symmetry preserving. Weak coupling instead produces an exciton insulator, degenerate with interlayer pairing, because of Fermi-surface nesting. The single-particle gap by itself does not tell these phases apart. Bilinear mean-field theory can find the ordered state and cannot represent the symmetric gapped state, which has no bilinear order parameter. Their spectral diagnostics, including where Green's-function zeros sit, are momentum-resolved and are not fixed by a local tunneling gap.

Lu et al. use the same bilayer setting as a materials-motivated parent state. At half filling the strong-coupling state is insulating and not superconducting. Doping destroys that filling-enforced state and produces interlayer spin-singlet superconductivity, with a strong-coupling transition-temperature peak near quarter filling. Their Hamiltonian keeps a single nearest-neighbor hopping and sets other band-structure details aside. Exciton order in this family folds the Fermi surface and relies on nesting, so a deformation of the Fermi-surface shape is a separate author-side test, not a result already computed in that paper.

The solver is not told these conclusions. The public curves are generated by `code/public_spectra.py` at \(t_2=0.2t_1\) and \(J=8t_1\). They are noisy traces of those calculations, not a fit to a named compound. The microscopic Hamiltonian, the material name, and words such as the lattice type or superconductivity stay on the author side.

## Research question

What microscopic mechanism accounts for a fourfold layered oxide whose spectra at one electron per metal site are gapped, whose high-temperature photoemission follows a square-lattice dispersion with both nearest-neighbor and next-nearest-neighbor hopping, and what low-temperature photoemission does that mechanism predict at $\nu=1/2$, at $\nu\approx 0.3$, and at $\nu\approx 0.2$?

This is the current synthesis of the idea frontier. Material refinements must create or update pages under `../wiki/ideas/` rather than erase the search history.

## Objective

From the public curves, all computed at one $(t_1,t_2,J)$, construct an explicit model, a filling--temperature phase diagram, and the low-temperature angle-resolved photoemission at $\nu=1/2$, at $\nu\approx 0.3$, and at $\nu\approx 0.2$, without being given the microscopic Hamiltonian. The Green's-function zeros at $\nu=1/2$ are not half the Brillouin zone. $\omega=0$ is the low-temperature chemical potential, which for this insulator is the midpoint of the gap.

The investigation must separate given facts from predictions, and it must show which mechanisms those facts rule out.

## Expected outcomes

- The three public facts leave more than one gapped, non-superconducting mechanism viable. A unique phase is not implied by the prompt alone.
- A model that starts from a Hubbard interaction, or from a bilinear mean-field decoupling, will tend to report symmetry breaking. That report is informative when it conflicts with the half-filled nuclear relaxation rate, which falls on cooling, or with the resonance remaining a single line on each crystal, or with a later ARPES prediction, and it is incomplete when the single-particle gap stays open while every bilinear correlator is short ranged.
- An interaction-generated symmetric gap can fit the charge gap, the spin gap, and the absence of superconductivity, and it can predict broadened ARPES weight with Green's-function zeros rather than folded quasiparticle bands.
- An ARPES prediction that only restates the existence of a gap does not discriminate. Folding, coherence, and the pole-versus-zero structure are the useful content of the prediction.
- The high-temperature dispersion table is not in the prompt. The public route to the two hoppings is the high-temperature differential conductance at $\nu\approx 0.3$: its van Hove peak sits near $+1.7D$, which with the filling fixes $t_2/t_1 \approx 0.2$. That band is rigid in density, as a high-temperature metal is. The low-temperature tunneling traces are not a rigid copy of it. Half-filled photoemission is no longer an input. On the tunneling zero, the occupied poles of the public half-filled spectrum are $-7.3D$ at $(0,0)$, $-4.8D$ at $(1/2,0)$, and $-4.3D$ at $(1/2,1/2)$; the unoccupied poles are $+4.3D$, $+6.1D$ and $+7.0D$ at the same momenta, so both bands rise from $(0,0)$ to $(1/2,1/2)$ and are narrower than $\varepsilon(k)/2$. The photoemission at $\nu\approx 0.3$ is scored on features. A Bogoliubov–de Gennes spectrum plus broadening will not match the generating lineshape in detail, and that mismatch is not a miss. Credit a gap at every momentum, of the same order as the tunneling half-width, about $2.7$. The minimum gap lies inside the bare Fermi surface, toward the minimum of the high-temperature band, because pair binding has pulled the chemical potential down; it does not sit on the original Fermi momentum. There is no sharp Bogoliubov back-bending. At high energy some weight remains from the half-filled symmetric gap. The momentum sum is the public tunneling curve: a flat bottom near zero, a gradual onset, weight spread to the band edges, and no sharp coherence peak. It is not a pseudogap and not a zero surface. At $\nu\approx 0.2$ credit sharp Bogoliubov coherence peaks and back-bending. The minimum gap lies on the bare Fermi surface and is of the same order as the tunneling half-width, about $1.7$. The momentum sum is that filling's public tunneling curve, including the sharp coherence peaks and the van Hove peak near $+3.7$. Giving $\nu\approx 0.2$ the broadened, back-bending-free spectrum of $\nu\approx 0.3$, or the reverse, is a miss. Green's-function zeros are the half-filled prediction.
